Today, as I write, Levent Alpöge, an employee at Anthropic, announced a proof, generated by an experimental unreleased version of Claude, of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, an open conjecture since 1859, considered the Holy Grail of mathematics.
The hypothesis states that all the non-trivial zeros or roots of the Riemann zeta function are on the complex line with the real part equal to 1/2.

Here’s the Riemann zeta function:  
\\[
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^s}=1+\frac{1}{2^s}+\frac{1}{3^s}+\frac{1}{4^s}+\frac{1}{5^s}+\cdots
\\]

Now, that’s great and all, but I don’t see any primes in that formula. Where’s the famous connection to primes? And why was Riemann even looking at such a function?

## Euler’s Product Formula

1737


\\[
\zeta(s)=\prod_{p}\left(1-p^{-s}\right)^{-1}
\\]




\\[
\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{1-2^{-s}}\frac{1}{1-3^{-s}}\frac{1}{1-5^{-s}}\frac{1}{1-7^{-s}}\cdots
\\]
